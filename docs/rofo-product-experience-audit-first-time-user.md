# Rofo Product Experience Audit: First-Time User Perspective

This audit evaluates Rofo as a first-time business user searching for commercial space. It is a product experience audit, not an engineering, UI polish, or architecture review.

Read first:

- `docs/product/rofo-master-plan.md`
- `docs/commercial-geography-model.md`

Reviewed public journey surfaces:

- Homepage
- City and market pages
- Building pages and Building Profiles
- Search Profile entry
- Recommendation results
- Example Location Brief
- Expert review and live market investigation handoff
- Thank-you and confirmation path
- Primary navigation and content organization

## Executive Summary

Rofo's underlying product strategy is strong and unusually differentiated: it starts with business fit, commercial geography, district tradeoffs, representative buildings, and Location Briefs instead of starting with available listings. The current public experience communicates much of that direction, especially through phrases such as "fit, not listings," "initial location analysis," "recommended starting path," and "representative examples."

The main product issue is not absence of value. It is that the value is still too easy to mistake for a better-written commercial real estate search site. A first-time visitor can understand that Rofo helps with commercial space, but may not immediately understand that Rofo's real deliverable is a Location Brief: a structured, explainable recommendation that tells them where to begin, why, what to compare, what tradeoffs matter, and what should be validated before touring.

The strongest product object, the Location Brief, appears in the experience, but it is not yet dominant enough as the promise, payoff, and conversion bridge. "Start Your Search" remains the primary call to action across the journey, while "Location Brief" is sometimes presented as an example or secondary artifact. That weakens the difference between Rofo and LoopNet, brokerage websites, Google, or AI chat.

Representative buildings are one of Rofo's clearest proof mechanisms. The current experience explains that they are not availability and that they help users understand a district. That is good. The opportunity is to make them more central earlier in the journey as evidence, not just examples. They should help the user believe: "Rofo understands the kind of commercial environment I need."

The lead handoff has improved because it is framed as optional expert review and live market investigation rather than only a broker inquiry. However, the transition can still feel like a form after a recommendation rather than the natural next step of a brief. The better product frame is: "Send this Location Brief for live market review."

## Current Journey

### Homepage

The homepage opens with a strong direction: "A smarter way to search for commercial space for your business." The supporting copy correctly explains that the user should compare markets, understand tradeoffs, and avoid touring the wrong buildings.

What works:

- The page starts with location strategy rather than inventory.
- The "Begin with fit, not listings" section is highly aligned with the master plan.
- The three-step explanation is simple: requirements, recommended areas, representative buildings.
- Trust proof exists through company history, usage, and recognition.

Where it falls short:

- The exact deliverable is still not concrete enough in the first 10 seconds.
- "Start Your Search" is familiar but generic. It could belong to any listings site.
- "Commercial space" and "commercial real estate" still compete with the stronger promise of "a Location Brief for your business."
- The user has to infer what they will receive: a recommendation page, a brief, an expert review, or listings.

### City Pages

City pages are strategically aligned with Rofo's product direction. They tell users to compare districts before listings and include area comparisons, guides, representative buildings, and expert help.

What works:

- The hero says: "Before comparing listings, make sure the search is focused on the right commercial areas."
- The page is organized around user questions: where should I look, what should I expect, how do I get the deal done.
- Representative buildings are explicitly positioned as examples, not live availability.
- City pages can support both SEO discovery and product entry.

Where it falls short:

- The page tries to do many jobs at once: market hub, district guide, recommendation entry, leasing education, representative building browsing, local help, and common searches.
- "Example Buildings," "Common searches," "Market context," "Rofo Insight," and "Commercial Leasing Guide" are all useful, but the hierarchy can become cognitively expensive.
- Users who arrive from search may still behave like they are browsing a city guide rather than entering an advisory workflow.
- The "Local Help" section risks sounding like a broker transition unless it is tied more tightly to the Location Brief.

### Building Pages

Building pages are one of Rofo's strongest proof surfaces. Building Profiles explain fit, tradeoffs, district relationships, validation questions, related comparisons, and why the building appears on Rofo.

What works:

- Representative Building pages explicitly say they are decision support, not ads for available space.
- "Why businesses evaluate this building" is a strong product framing.
- Fit, tradeoffs, validation questions, nearby alternatives, and district context demonstrate expertise.
- Building Profiles make abstract district recommendations feel real.

Where it falls short:

- A first-time user may land on a building page and not understand how it connects to the recommendation product.
- "Start Your Search" from a building page can feel like leaving the building context instead of using that building as input.
- Building pages should more clearly say: "Use this as a reference point in your Location Brief."
- Representative buildings may be interpreted as listings or inventory despite disclaimers unless the product repeatedly explains their evidence role.

### Search Profile Entry

The Search Profile is reasonably short and aligned with the principle that Rofo should capture only enough to start. It asks for location, space type, people or size, timing, workspace style, important factors, and features.

What works:

- The form is progressive rather than one large intake.
- The location-intent question is strategically valuable: focus here, compare nearby markets, or recommend the best markets.
- Contact capture is positioned as saving the profile and viewing recommendations.
- Phone is optional and explicitly framed as optional.

Where it falls short:

- The user is not always told why each question matters.
- The form asks for contact details before the recommendation payoff in some flows, which can create lead-form anxiety.
- "Location Profile" is internally coherent, but "Get My Recommendations" or "Get My Location Brief" is more concrete for a first-time user.
- Some questions may be postponable until after the first recommendation, especially detailed features or workspace style.

### Recommendation Results

The recommendation page is the most product-rich part of the journey. It includes search recap, recommended starting path, rationale, confidence, tradeoffs, best-fit users, alternatives, representative buildings, refinement, and optional expert review.

What works:

- It correctly says results are not live listings.
- "Start with the strongest fit, then pressure-test alternatives" is excellent.
- Tradeoffs and alternatives are visible, which makes the recommendation feel credible.
- Representative buildings are shown in context and include why they matter, best fit, and tradeoff.
- The page offers refinement after the first direction is visible.

Where it falls short:

- It may feel like a long analysis page rather than a single coherent brief.
- The term "Initial Location Analysis" competes with "Location Brief."
- The representative building section could be closer to the primary recommendation, because it is one of the fastest trust builders.
- The confidence indicator can help, but a public numeric match risks feeling like generic recommendation scoring unless the evidence is dominant.
- The final expert-review form has many options, which may be appropriate for motivated users but can slow momentum.

### Example Location Brief

The example brief is a strong product artifact and should do more work across the funnel.

What works:

- It gives the user a concrete preview of the output.
- It shows the relationship between business requirements, recommended market path, tradeoffs, validation questions, representative buildings, and expert review.
- It makes Rofo feel more like an advisory product than a listings portal.

Where it falls short:

- It is secondary in the journey rather than central to the promise.
- The example uses generic building placeholders rather than named representative buildings, which reduces proof.
- It reads as a sample document but could do more to demonstrate the exact transformation from user input to recommendation.

### Lead Handoff

The handoff is better than a conventional brokerage lead form because it is framed as optional expert review and live market investigation. It asks what Rofo should investigate and allows users to keep representative buildings as reference points.

What works:

- "Request expert review when you are ready" lowers pressure.
- "Research first; contact me with findings" is a strong option.
- The handoff asks for availability, comps, market insight, and broker guidance separately.
- It preserves the user's recommendation context.

Where it falls short:

- The final state can still feel like a lead capture step rather than a continuation of the Location Brief.
- The thank-you page says Rofo will follow up with "relevant options," which sounds more like listings than an expert review of the brief.
- The transition should reinforce: "Your brief has been received for live market review."
- The form asks the user to choose investigation details at a moment when they may prefer Rofo to recommend next steps.

## Strengths

- Rofo has a clear strategic wedge: location-first commercial real estate.
- The product correctly focuses on where to search before what to tour.
- District comparisons and tradeoffs make recommendations more credible.
- Representative buildings are a strong explanation layer.
- Building Profiles demonstrate real commercial judgment.
- The Search Profile is short enough to support first-time engagement.
- The recommendation page gives more substance than a generic result list.
- Expert review is optional and can be framed as a natural continuation.
- The Location Brief is the right canonical product object.
- The geography model gives Rofo a scalable way to organize national expansion.

## Weaknesses

- The first 10 seconds do not make the Location Brief concrete enough.
- "Start Your Search" is too generic for Rofo's differentiated product.
- Several pages mix exploration, education, proof, lead capture, and SEO content.
- Representative buildings are still too easy to read as examples rather than evidence.
- The user may not understand what happens after submitting contact information.
- "Initial Location Analysis," "Location Profile," "Location Brief," "Live Market Investigation," "Representative examples," and "Expert Review" can feel like several product objects instead of one journey.
- Numeric match and confidence language may feel generic unless tied directly to visible evidence.
- The recommendation output can feel long before it feels complete.
- Thank-you language underplays the advisory artifact and returns to generic "options" language.

## Major Product Risks

1. Rofo may be mistaken for a commercial listings portal with better copy.

   The experience says Rofo is different, but generic CTAs and some "available spaces/options" language pull users back toward a listing-site mental model.

2. Users may not understand the payoff before investing effort.

   The product asks for a profile, but the exact deliverable should be more concrete before the first click.

3. The most credible evidence may appear too late.

   District reasoning and representative buildings are the proof. They should appear earlier and be explicitly connected to trust.

4. Lead handoff may weaken trust if it feels like a brokerage form.

   Rofo earns trust through analysis. The handoff should preserve that by centering the brief, not the contact request.

5. Internal terminology may leak into public comprehension.

   Users do not need to understand the internal distinction between Search Profile, Initial Location Analysis, Location Brief, and Live Market Investigation. They need to know what they will receive and what happens next.

6. City pages may dilute the product story.

   City pages are valuable, but if every module has similar weight, first-time users may browse instead of understanding Rofo's guided path.

## Section 1: The Product Promise

### What a visitor can understand within 10 seconds

A visitor can probably understand:

- Rofo helps with commercial space search.
- Rofo starts with location fit before listings.
- Rofo gives recommendations and examples.

A visitor may not immediately understand:

- The output is a Location Brief.
- The brief is more valuable than a list of buildings.
- Rofo has structured commercial geography and building evidence behind the output.
- The user can get value before broker outreach.
- Rofo is meaningfully different from a good brokerage website with educational content.

### Recommended promise

Rofo should lead with the concrete artifact and outcome:

```text
Get a Location Brief for your commercial space search.
Rofo recommends where to begin, why those districts fit, what to compare, and which buildings explain the market before you tour.
```

This makes four things clear:

- what Rofo does
- what the user receives
- why the experience is different
- why representative buildings matter

## Section 2: Information Architecture

The current IA has the right ingredients, but many pages present too many equally weighted choices. A first-time user should not have to decide whether they are searching markets, reading a guide, browsing example buildings, comparing districts, starting a profile, viewing an example brief, or contacting an expert.

Recommended progressive disclosure:

1. Promise: Get a Location Brief.
2. Inputs: Tell Rofo the basics.
3. Output: Recommended districts, tradeoffs, representative buildings, validation questions.
4. Proof: Example brief and sample searches.
5. Exploration: City, district, and building pages for users who want context.
6. Handoff: Send the brief for live market review.

City pages should still support exploration and SEO, but the primary product path should be unmistakable: "Use this market to create a Location Brief."

## Section 3: Recommendation Experience

The Search Profile asks reasonable questions, but the experience should more clearly explain why each question improves the brief.

Question-by-question product assessment:

| Question | User Clarity | Product Value | Recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Location | High | High | Keep first. Explain that Rofo can focus, compare nearby markets, or discover better options. |
| Location intent | Medium | High | Keep. This is strategically important and differentiating. |
| Space type | High | High | Keep early. It materially changes districts and building examples. |
| People or size | High | High | Keep early. It shapes building format and district fit. |
| Timing | Medium | Medium | Keep, but can be lighter. It matters more for live availability than initial location fit. |
| Workspace style | Medium | Medium | Consider moving after first recommendation unless it materially changes ranking. |
| Important factors | Medium | High | Keep, but explain that these shape tradeoffs and alternatives. |
| Features | Medium | Medium | Move later or make optional. Detailed requirements are better for expert review unless essential. |
| Contact | Low to Medium | Business-critical | Tie explicitly to saving or reviewing the Location Brief. Avoid making it feel like payment for results. |

The core pacing should be:

```text
Minimum viable profile
First Location Brief
Refinement
Expert review
```

## Section 4: Recommendation Deliverable

The recommendation page has substantial value. It explains why a starting point fits, what alternatives matter, where confidence comes from, and what to validate. It should feel less like a web page of sections and more like a delivered work product.

Recommended structure:

1. Brief summary: "Here is where we would start and why."
2. Recommended path: primary district plus two alternatives.
3. Evidence: representative buildings that explain the recommendation.
4. Tradeoffs: what could make the top recommendation wrong.
5. Validation checklist: what to confirm before tours.
6. Expert review: send this brief for live market investigation.

The user should leave thinking:

```text
Rofo gave me a credible first opinion, not a list.
```

## Section 5: Representative Buildings

Representative buildings should play a larger product role. They are not merely examples; they are proof that Rofo understands the commercial environment behind a recommendation.

Current strengths:

- The experience clarifies that representative buildings are not current availability.
- Building cards explain why a building matters, best fit, and tradeoff.
- Building Profiles support district understanding.

Recommended changes:

- Introduce representative buildings earlier in the recommendation summary.
- Frame them as "buildings Rofo uses to explain this district."
- Show one or two buildings directly beside the primary recommendation.
- Add a simple line: "These are reference buildings, not listings."
- On Building Profiles, let users add or preserve the building as a reference point in their brief.

Representative buildings should support trust by making the abstract recommendation concrete.

## Section 6: Lead Experience

The current handoff is close to the right model but needs sharper continuity.

Current issue:

The user receives a recommendation, then encounters "Optional Expert Review" and "Live Market Investigation." These are reasonable terms, but the form can still feel like a separate lead flow.

Recommended handoff:

```text
Send this Location Brief for live market review.
```

Supporting copy:

```text
Rofo will use your brief to check current availability, comparable buildings, timing, and local tradeoffs. You can ask for research first, broker guidance, or both.
```

This preserves user control and makes the handoff feel like a continuation of the recommendation experience.

The thank-you page should confirm the artifact:

```text
Your Location Brief has been received for review.
We will follow up with market findings tied to your recommended districts, representative buildings, and requirements.
```

## Section 8: Competitive Positioning

Rofo's advantages are real but should be demonstrated more directly.

### Versus LoopNet

LoopNet answers: "What spaces are listed?"

Rofo should answer: "Where should this business look first, and why?"

### Versus CoStar

CoStar is data-rich but not built for first-time tenant decision clarity.

Rofo should emphasize plain-language recommendations, district tradeoffs, and a brief the user can act on.

### Versus a brokerage website

Brokerage sites often convert users into inquiries quickly.

Rofo should feel like it gives value before asking for the handoff.

### Versus Google

Google returns pages and fragments.

Rofo should provide a structured recommendation with district logic and representative buildings.

### Versus AI chat

AI chat can answer loosely but may not be grounded in Rofo's commercial geography, curated evidence, and building profiles.

Rofo should show its evidence. That is the trust advantage.

## Lessons Learned

Rofo is already saying many of the right things. The gap is consistency and product dominance. The backend has matured into a commercial-location intelligence system, but the frontend still occasionally asks users to assemble the product story from several pages and terms.

The fastest path to clarity is not more explanation. It is making the deliverable concrete:

```text
Rofo creates a Location Brief that recommends where your business should begin its commercial space search.
```

Everything else should support that promise.

## Recommended Next Highest-Leverage Improvement

Make the Location Brief the primary user-facing product object everywhere a first-time user encounters Rofo.

The focused next sprint should update public journey language and hierarchy so the homepage, Search Profile, recommendations page, expert handoff, and thank-you state all communicate one continuous journey:

```text
Tell Rofo the basics.
Get a Location Brief.
Use representative buildings and tradeoffs to understand the market.
Send the brief for live market review when ready.
```
