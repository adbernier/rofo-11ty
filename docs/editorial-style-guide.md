# Rofo Editorial Style Guide

Rofo helps people make better commercial location decisions.

This guide is a practical writing standard for Rofo city pages, district pages, Building Briefs, handbook content, recommendation pages, and product copy. It should be used when creating new content and when polishing existing content.

## Editorial Philosophy

Rofo is not:

- a brokerage website
- a listing portal
- a marketing brochure
- an architectural history site

Rofo is:

- an editorial commercial location guide
- a decision-support product
- commercially intelligent
- balanced
- honest about tradeoffs
- written by people who understand commercial real estate

The writing should feel like advice from an experienced tenant representative. It should help a business owner or operator understand what matters, what to compare, and what to validate before spending time on the wrong buildings or districts.

## Voice

Rofo should sound:

- calm
- confident
- knowledgeable
- concise
- specific
- practical
- unbiased
- human

Rofo should never sound:

- promotional
- exaggerated
- sales-driven
- AI-generated
- corporate
- academic

Prefer short sentences when the decision is important. Use longer sentences only when they clarify a tradeoff.

## Grammar Standards

Use correct articles throughout all content.

Use:

- an office building
- an established headquarters
- a creative workplace
- a campus environment
- a lower-rise office setting
- an adaptable floorplate

Do not write awkward noun stacks to avoid choosing an article. If a sentence sounds unnatural when read aloud, rewrite it.

## District Names

Use natural English for district names.

Use:

- the Financial District
- the Mission District
- the Design District
- Mission Bay
- SoMa
- Jackson Square

Generally avoid:

- the SoMa
- the Mission Bay

Use an article only when it is natural in normal speech.

## Building Names

Use official or established building names consistently.

Use:

- Salesforce Tower
- 555 California
- 101 California
- Levi's Plaza
- The Exchange

Avoid unnecessary repetition. Once the building is established, use "the building" where it reads naturally.

## Commercial Vocabulary

Use language commercial real estate professionals and tenants actually understand.

Avoid invented editorial labels in public copy:

- executive benchmark
- innovation benchmark
- creative benchmark
- flagship opportunity
- destination building
- premier environment
- premier office
- world-class
- unparalleled
- best-in-class

Prefer:

- landmark office tower
- established office building
- modern headquarters tower
- boutique office building
- campus-style office environment
- creative office building
- corporate office tower
- mixed-use office building
- Class A office tower, when supported
- lower-rise office campus
- transit-oriented downtown office building

Do not invent categories that are not commonly understood.

## Words and Phrases to Avoid

Avoid brokerage, brochure, and generic AI language:

- offers
- boasts
- features
- premier
- ideal
- unparalleled
- exceptional
- cutting-edge
- state-of-the-art
- dynamic
- vibrant
- world-class
- highly desirable
- prestigious
- unlock
- ecosystem, unless a real business ecosystem is being discussed
- commercial fabric
- market context, when a simpler phrase works
- representative form
- reads as

Prefer direct alternatives:

- has
- includes
- works well for
- may fit
- businesses choose it because
- compare it with
- validate
- tradeoff
- established
- modern
- lower-rise
- client-facing
- transit-oriented

## Opening Sentence Standard

The first sentence should explain why the building, district, or topic matters.

Weak:

> 345 California is an executive benchmark in Financial District.

Strong:

> 345 California is a landmark office tower in the Financial District.

Better:

> 345 California is one of the Financial District's classic corporate office towers.

The opening sentence should always answer:

Why should someone care?

For Building Briefs, the opening should quickly establish the building's commercial identity, not just its address or architecture.

## Decision-Oriented Writing

Every section should help answer a decision.

Instead of only describing:

- What is this?
- Where is it?
- What does it look like?

Prefer:

- Who is this right for?
- What tradeoff exists?
- When would another building be better?
- What should be validated before shortlisting?
- Which district or building should be compared next?

Rofo content should move the user toward a clearer next decision.

## Comparisons

Comparisons should reflect real leasing decisions.

Avoid comparing buildings or districts simply because they are nearby.

Prefer alternatives that clarify a practical choice:

- same district, different level of formality
- same user profile, different cost or access pattern
- traditional tower versus creative office
- central transit versus parking or freeway access
- flagship address versus quieter workplace
- modern district identity versus established downtown services

If a comparison does not teach the user something, leave it out.

## Tradeoffs

Every Building Brief should acknowledge tradeoffs.

Rofo should never imply every building is appropriate for every business. Tradeoffs build credibility and help the reader self-select.

Strong tradeoffs are specific:

- Strong client-facing address, but likely higher occupancy cost.
- Good freeway access, but weaker BART convenience.
- Historic character, but floorplate efficiency may need validation.
- Modern headquarters signal, but excessive for smaller teams.

Weak tradeoffs are vague:

- May not fit everyone.
- Parking can be limited.
- Costs may vary.

## Editorial Checklist

Before publishing any page, confirm:

1. Grammar is natural.
2. Articles are correct.
3. District names read naturally.
4. Building names are consistent.
5. Public copy avoids invented terminology.
6. Brokerage marketing language has been removed.
7. The opening sentence immediately explains relevance.
8. Comparisons are commercially meaningful.
9. Tradeoffs are honest and specific.
10. Unsupported claims are absent.
11. The page reads smoothly aloud.

## Building Brief Checklist

Before publishing a Building Brief, confirm:

1. The Rofo Take explains why the building matters.
2. Best Fit and May Not Fit help a tenant self-identify.
3. Advantages and tradeoffs are not duplicates.
4. Validation questions would be useful on a real tour.
5. Location Context explains how the district affects the decision.
6. Comparisons point to real alternatives, not just nearby addresses.
7. The page does not imply current availability.
8. Snapshot labels use plain commercial vocabulary.
