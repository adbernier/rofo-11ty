# Business-Side Commercial Real Estate Opportunity Study

Research current through August 14, 2026.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented fact** — directly supported by cited research or product documentation.
- **Research inference** — conclusion drawn across multiple sources.
- **Strategic hypothesis** — plausible opportunity that requires validation with Rofo’s target users and data.

## 1. Executive Summary

Commercial real-estate technology is not devoid of occupier products. It is unevenly distributed.

Large enterprises and multi-location brands can buy sophisticated portfolio planning, lease administration, workplace utilization, demographic modeling, and site-selection systems. Brokers, landlords, and investors have strong inventory, transaction, workflow, and property-intelligence platforms. But businesses without a corporate-real-estate team still encounter CRE as a collection of listings, professionals, documents, spreadsheets, municipal databases, and deadlines.

The structurally underserved job is:

> Continuously translating a changing business into a defensible real-estate strategy—and then carrying that context through location choice, property evaluation, negotiation, occupancy, renewal, and expansion.

The major gaps are not simply “better listings”:

1. **Requirement formation.** Businesses often begin with a rough size, rent, and preferred neighborhood. They discover workforce, operating, technical, regulatory, buildout, and flexibility requirements only while evaluating properties.

2. **Location reasoning before inventory.** Office users need to reconcile workforce geography and workplace purpose; retailers need customer demand and unit economics; industrial users need infrastructure, labor, freight, power, loading, zoning, and environmental compatibility. Most general marketplaces begin with a map and current availability, not the operating model.

3. **Translation across domains.** The decision spans operations, finance, HR, facilities, construction, legal, and local regulation. No single participant normally owns the whole truth.

4. **Comparable economics.** Asking rent is not total occupancy cost. Concessions, operating expenses, taxes, utilities, parking, buildout, downtime, moving costs, restoration obligations, and flexibility can reverse an apparent rent advantage.

5. **Fit verification.** A listing can describe a building without proving that a particular business can legally, technically, economically, and practically operate there.

6. **Decision continuity.** Search artifacts, rejected alternatives, assumptions, lease obligations, and actual location performance are rarely preserved in a reusable business memory.

7. **The interval between transactions.** Critical dates, utilization, changing employee or customer geography, excess capacity, expansion needs, and market conditions continue after lease execution, but small and midsize occupiers commonly revert to calendars and spreadsheets.

Modern AI materially improves requirement interviews, document extraction, cross-source synthesis, scenario construction, explanation, monitoring, and memory. It does not solve unavailable or unreliable facts. CRE remains constrained by incomplete availability, proprietary effective-rent comps, inconsistent zoning, thin building-system data, and private buildout and operating-cost information. Deloitte found that only 14% of surveyed real-estate organizations believed they had well-structured data processes and robust privacy policies; data readiness and confidentiality were leading AI barriers. [Deloitte 2025 CRE Outlook](https://www2.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/financial-services/commercial-real-estate-outlook.html)

The central opportunity is therefore not a chatbot over listings. It is a context-and-decision layer connecting:

> business model → requirement → geography → physical and regulatory fit → economics → alternatives → transaction → occupied performance → next decision

That layer could serve a persistent relationship, but persistence must be earned through high-value events and monitoring. Most businesses will not maintain a “CRE dashboard” merely because one exists.

## 2. The Business CRE Journey

The journey is better represented as a recurring decision loop than a linear funnel.

| Stage | Business questions and required information | Current solution and participants | Friction and consequence of error |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Trigger recognition** | Are we running out of space? Is the location hurting recruiting, customers, production, or cost? Are renewal dates approaching? Requires headcount, utilization, sales, capacity, lease dates, operational complaints, and growth plans. | Founder, COO, CFO, HR, facilities; calendars, spreadsheets, anecdote, lease files. | Signals arrive in different departments. A late start weakens negotiating leverage and narrows options. |
| **2. Strategic choice** | Renew, relocate, expand, contract, add a site, consolidate, use flex space, or sublease? Requires scenario costs, business outlook, current lease rights, disruption cost, market alternatives, and timing. | Executives, broker, finance, workplace/facilities advisers. | Alternatives are evaluated with different assumptions. “Stay” appears cheap when avoided capex is visible but long-term mismatch is not—or vice versa. |
| **3. Requirement definition** | What activities, people, customers, equipment, inventory, utilities, access, and future options must the space support? | Internal interviews, broker questionnaires, architect/programmer, spreadsheets. | Users frequently express proxies—square feet, rent, ZIP code—rather than operational needs. Missing requirements surface during tours or diligence. |
| **4. Market/geography selection** | Which market, district, or trade area best serves labor, customers, suppliers, logistics, brand, regulation, and cost? | Broker advice, demographic/GIS tools, consultants, personal intuition. | Relevant data is fragmented; weighting tradeoffs is difficult. Businesses often default to familiar geography or executive preference. |
| **5. Inventory discovery** | What is actually available now or likely to become available? What is off-market, divisible, sublease, or deliverable in time? | LoopNet, Crexi, CoStar through professionals, brokerage surveys, local relationships. | Duplicate, stale, incomplete, and selectively marketed inventory; inconsistent fields; availability is not the same as deliverability. |
| **6. Shortlisting** | Which properties fit the hard constraints, and why? What compromises does each require? | Broker survey, maps, PDFs, email, spreadsheets. | Documents use inconsistent terminology. Technical and regulatory constraints are often absent from listing records. |
| **7. Tours and experiential evaluation** | Does access, layout, condition, context, noise, image, loading, parking, and surrounding environment work in practice? | Physical tours, notes, photos, broker memory. | Tour evidence becomes unstructured and incomparable; first impressions can dominate hard criteria. |
| **8. Technical and regulatory diligence** | Is the use permitted? Are power, floor load, clear height, HVAC, plumbing, ventilation, life safety, accessibility, environmental condition, telecom, and loading adequate? | Architect, engineer, contractor, attorney, municipality, landlord documents. | Expensive specialists enter late. Municipal records vary. A “possible” use may still require costly or uncertain approvals. |
| **9. Buildout and schedule validation** | What must change, who pays, how much, how long, and what could delay opening? | Architect, GC, landlord, project manager, preliminary estimates. | Costs are design- and condition-dependent; early estimates are uncertain. Delays can create double rent or lost revenue. |
| **10. Proposal and economic comparison** | What is the effective cost and risk of each alternative? What concessions and obligations matter? | Broker financial models, spreadsheets, LOIs, attorney review. | Base rent, NNN/CAM, escalations, TI, free rent, parking, utilities, guarantees, and restoration are easily compared inconsistently. |
| **11. Negotiation and lease review** | What is market, negotiable, enforceable, and risky? Who owns repairs and compliance? What flexibility is preserved? | Broker negotiates economics; attorney negotiates language; executives approve. | Market context, legal meaning, and operating consequences live with different advisers. Misaligned incentives may be invisible to the business. |
| **12. Buildout, move, and opening** | Are permits, construction, IT, furniture/equipment, insurance, inspections, and move dependencies on track? | Project manager, GC, architect, vendors, facilities, landlord. | Handoffs from transaction to delivery are weak; assumptions negotiated in the lease may not reach operators. |
| **13. Occupancy operations** | Is the location performing? Are costs correct? Is utilization, customer demand, capacity, or maintenance meeting expectations? | AP/accounting, facilities tools, workplace sensors, POS/ERP, service tickets. | Real-estate data is separated from business outcomes. Problems are observed without revisiting the original decision assumptions. |
| **14. Change management** | Do we need more/less space, adjacent space, a sublease, reconfiguration, or a second location? | Internal review, broker engagement, portfolio software for larger firms. | Recognition is late and institutional memory is weak. |
| **15. Renewal/disposition/next cycle** | When must we act? What leverage and alternatives exist? How did the location perform relative to expectations? | Calendar reminders, lease systems, broker outreach, attorney. | Missed notice dates can destroy options. Prior searches and rejected alternatives are rarely reusable. |

**Documented context:** In CBRE’s 2025 Americas office survey, renewals remained highly prevalent, while relocation motivations included flexibility, cost, amenities, employee location, and consolidation. CBRE explicitly stresses early planning because suitable well-located space can be constrained. [CBRE 2025 Occupier Survey](https://www.cbre.com/insights/reports/2025-americas-office-occupier-sentiment-survey)

**Research inference:** Small occupiers do not necessarily follow all 15 stages explicitly. They still incur the risks of those stages; they compress them into fewer informal decisions.

## 3. Requirement Intelligence

### Requirements by property type

| Type | Distinctive requirement logic |
|---|---|
| **Office** | Workplace purpose, attendance distribution rather than average headcount, team interaction, employee home geography, transit/parking, recruiting reach, amenities, security, IT, meeting mix, client image, and flexibility. |
| **Industrial** | Use classification, production process, power capacity and voltage, gas, water, floor load, clear height, column spacing, ventilation, hazardous materials, noise, yard, loading, truck circulation, labor, freight corridors, and expansion land. |
| **Warehouse/distribution** | Inventory velocity, pallet positions, clear height, dock/grade doors, trailer storage, throughput, automation, fire suppression, last-mile or regional reach, carrier access, congestion, and labor. |
| **Flex** | Ratio of office/lab/light-production/storage, loading, power, customer-facing quality, divisibility, and ability to change the mix. “Flex” is a marketing category, not proof of operational fit. |
| **Retail/restaurant** | Customer origin and behavior, visibility, access, traffic direction, parking, foot traffic, co-tenancy, competition, cannibalization, signage, delivery, use restrictions, exclusives, grease/hood utilities, patio, and sales-to-occupancy-cost ratio. |
| **Medical** | Patient geography, referral networks, accessibility, parking, transit, plumbing, backup power, imaging/equipment loads, medical waste, privacy, life safety, licensing, and compatible neighboring uses. |
| **Life science/R&D** | Lab classification, ventilation and air changes, chemical storage, loading, vibration, floor load, power redundancy, water/gases, waste handling, emergency systems, permitting, specialized buildout, and proximity to research talent/ecosystems. |
| **Special purpose** | Requirements are dominated by the operating process: education occupancy, childcare outdoor space, auto repair lifts and drainage, food production sanitation, cold storage refrigeration, data-center power/fiber/cooling, entertainment assembly limits, etc. |

### How requirements are actually formed

Businesses tend to fall into four groups:

1. **Repeat, scaled occupiers:** possess scorecards, prototype specifications, historical performance, professional teams, and approval workflows.
2. **Operationally sophisticated first-timers:** understand their business process but cannot readily translate it into CRE terminology.
3. **Broker-led occupiers:** form requirements through the options a broker surfaces.
4. **Listing-led first-timers:** begin with visible inventory and learn requirements reactively.

SiteZeus, Esri, Tango, Buxton, and Locate demonstrate what is possible for scaled retail and service networks: customer profiling, trade areas, performance forecasting, cannibalization, portfolio context, and site scoring. For example, Esri combines demographic, spending, business, and lifestyle data with trade-area and site-selection analysis, while SiteZeus trains models on existing-location performance. [Esri Business Analyst](https://www.esri.com/en-us/arcgis/products/arcgis-business-analyst/overview), [SiteZeus](https://sitezeus.com/products/synergy)

**Research inference:** Sophisticated requirement intelligence exists where a company has many sites and outcomes from which to learn. It is much weaker for a first or second location, where historical company data is thin and advisory costs are harder to justify.

### The key distinction: stated versus latent requirements

- **Stated:** “5,000 square feet, under $30/SF, near downtown.”
- **Latent:** “Twenty employees commute from three directions; peak attendance is Tuesday; customers visit twice daily; equipment requires 400A three-phase power; the company may double in 30 months; opening must occur before a contract starts.”

Technology can help discover latent requirements, but only if the interview is adaptive and explains why questions matter. A long static questionnaire will be abandoned.

## 4. Information Asymmetry

### Information genuinely unavailable to the ordinary business

- True real-time availability, especially off-market, shadow, future, or informally divisible space.
- Effective lease economics and concessions on comparable executed transactions.
- Other active requirements competing for the same space.
- Landlord reservation price, urgency, credit concerns, and internal approvals.
- Building-system condition not recently inspected.
- Accurate buildout cost before design and investigation.
- Future neighboring tenancy, ownership strategy, or redevelopment plans.
- Informal local knowledge and relationship history.

CoStar’s scale—property records, availabilities, tenants, lease activity, comps, market analytics—illustrates both the value of verified CRE data and how much important information resides in professional paid systems. [CoStar products](https://www.costar.com/products)

### Information technically available but inaccessible

- Municipal zoning codes, use tables, overlays, conditional-use processes, and permit histories.
- Lease language governing options, pass-throughs, maintenance, insurance, assignment, guarantees, restoration, exclusives, and co-tenancy.
- Taxes, assessments, utility tariffs, local incentives, flood/environmental information, and accessibility obligations.
- Employee/customer geography and drive/transit-time analyses.
- Building plans, certificates, equipment schedules, and engineering reports.
- Comparable proposals spread across PDFs and emails.

The SBA’s location guidance makes clear that location determines zoning, licensing, taxes, and operating costs, but leaves the business to navigate local agencies. [SBA location guidance](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/pick-your-business-location)

### Structurally interpretive information

Some facts are available but do not answer the business question without translation:

- A zoning code does not establish that a proposed use will be approved.
- A power figure does not establish that equipment and expansion can be supported.
- Asking rent does not establish total occupancy cost.
- Foot traffic does not establish likely store sales.
- Employee proximity does not establish whether staff will attend.
- A lease clause summary does not determine legal or commercial acceptability.
- A building amenity list does not establish workplace effectiveness.

**Research inference:** The deepest asymmetry is not only that professionals know more. It is that the business cannot easily connect facts across professional boundaries.

## 5. Common Decision Failures

| Failure | Why it occurs | Preventability by technology |
|---|---|---|
| Beginning with listings before strategy | Inventory is concrete; requirements and geography are abstract. | **High:** guided requirement and geography reasoning. |
| Optimizing base rent | Other costs are scattered or uncertain. | **High–medium:** standardized total-cost scenarios, with uncertainty ranges. |
| Choosing an executive-convenient location | Employee/customer/logistics data is not assembled. | **High:** origin analysis and explicit tradeoffs. |
| Using average headcount for office sizing | Hybrid attendance is uneven by day and team. | **Medium:** utilization scenarios; policy uncertainty remains human. |
| Over- or under-leasing | Growth forecasts are treated as point estimates. | **Medium:** scenario and flexibility analysis. |
| Underestimating buildout and schedule | Technical validation happens late; early scope is vague. | **Medium:** benchmark and risk flags, not a substitute for professionals. |
| Assuming permitted use | Listing descriptions and landlord statements are mistaken for approval. | **High for flagging; low for final verification.** |
| Selecting technically unsuitable industrial/flex space | Important fields are absent or terminology is misunderstood. | **High for screening; medium for final validation.** |
| Comparing proposals inconsistently | Different structures, escalation bases, and concessions. | **High:** document extraction and normalization. |
| Missing lease dates or obligations | Lease becomes a static PDF after signing. | **Very high:** extraction, reminders, responsibility mapping. |
| Renewing without market alternatives | Process begins after leverage has eroded. | **High:** proactive monitoring and early scenario refresh. |
| Failing to preserve decision history | Emails and spreadsheets disappear with participants. | **High:** structured decisions, assumptions, and rejection reasons. |
| Confusing prediction with certainty | Model precision obscures source limitations. | **High:** provenance, confidence, missing-data disclosure, and counterfactuals. |

Practitioner accounts repeatedly describe surprises involving use approval, HVAC responsibility, rentable versus usable area, and buildout overruns. These anecdotes are not prevalence estimates, but they demonstrate how ordinary businesses encounter cross-domain failures. [NYC commercial leasing guide](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/sbs/downloads/pdf/about/reports/commercial-lease-guide-accessible.pdf)

## 6. Current CRE Product Landscape

| Category and representative products | Fundamental orientation | Genuine strengths | Occupier-side limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Listings/marketplaces:** LoopNet/CoStar, Crexi, CommercialSearch, PropertyShark | Broker, owner, investor; secondarily searcher | Inventory exposure, property discovery, contacts, maps, lead generation, market/property records. | Usually begins with properties; weak at discovering the business requirement and carrying it through occupancy. |
| **Professional market data:** CoStar, CompStak, Reonomy, Cherre | CRE professional, investor, broker, data team | Verified property records, ownership, tenants, comps, aggregation, data normalization. | Cost, professional vocabulary, and raw-data orientation limit accessibility for occasional occupiers. |
| **Landlord leasing/asset management:** VTS, MRI, Yardi | Landlord, asset manager, property manager | Pipeline, stacking plans, proposals, leasing economics, asset operations, tenant experience. | The business is commonly represented as a requirement or prospect in the landlord’s workflow. |
| **Investment/deal management:** Dealpath, Altrio and related platforms | Investor, developer, lender | Sourcing, underwriting, diligence, approvals, proprietary deal history. | Strong decision systems, but for capital deployment rather than business occupancy. |
| **Tenant lease/accounting:** LeaseQuery, Visual Lease, Occupier | Finance and corporate real-estate teams | Accounting compliance, abstraction, critical dates, obligations, portfolio records. | Often valuable after a lease exists; implementations assume organizational process and recurring portfolio scale. |
| **Occupier lifecycle/IWMS:** Tango, MRI, Planon, Eptura | Enterprise occupier/facilities/workplace team | Lease, site selection, portfolio scenarios, space utilization, projects, maintenance, workplace operations. | Comprehensive but enterprise-oriented; setup, data, and process burden are disproportionate for smaller businesses. |
| **Site/location intelligence:** Esri, Placer.ai, Buxton, SiteZeus, Tango, AlphaMap | Analyst, retailer, developer, broker, investor | Demographics, customer behavior, foot traffic, trade areas, forecasts, cannibalization, mapping. | Powerful tools often require expertise, proprietary business outcomes, or scaled expansion programs. |
| **Brokerage workflow:** Buildout, Apto/Propertybase, ClientLook, VTS, tour/proposal tools | Broker and brokerage management | CRM, prospecting, marketing, surveys, tour books, deal coordination. | Improves service production but does not inherently give the business independent decision memory or transparency. |
| **AI-enhanced incumbents:** CoStar/Visual Lease, Crexi AI, Yardi Virtuoso, Occupier AI, Dealpath AI, CBRE Ellis AI, JLL GPT/Falcon | Existing professional customer | Document intake, extraction, search, summaries, proprietary-data querying, content and workflow automation. | Mostly accelerates the incumbent’s established job and user orientation. |
| **AI-integrated/AI-native:** Locate, SiteZeus/Zeus.ai, Parcyl, Sytes/Rio and emerging research/feasibility agents | Mostly multi-unit brands, brokers, developers, or deal teams | Natural-language analysis, site scoring, document ingestion, feasibility automation, requirement matching. | Promising, but narrow vertical focus, limited public validation, dependence on proprietary data, and continuing human execution. |

Examples of actual—not merely marketed—AI functionality include:

- Occupier summarizes clauses and maps landlord/tenant responsibilities. [Occupier AI](https://www.occupier.com/product/ai-lease-management)
- Visual Lease extracts and validates lease terms against CoStar data. [Visual Lease AI abstraction](https://visuallease.com/solutions/ai-lease-abstraction/)
- Dealpath extracts offering memoranda, recommends comps, and grounds natural-language analysis in proprietary investment data. [Dealpath AI](https://www.dealpath.com/ai/)
- Crexi extracts listing documents, enriches properties, generates deal materials, and answers market questions with cited data. [Crexi AI](https://www.crexi.com/ai)
- Yardi Smart Lease writes reviewed, source-linked abstractions into operational lease records. [Yardi Smart Lease](https://www.yardi.com/product/smart-lease/)
- Locate combines brand-specific site models with brokerage execution. [Locate](https://locate.ai/)

### What should not be rebuilt

The market already has substantial capability in:

- Broad professional property databases.
- Generic listing publishing and lead capture.
- Landlord leasing pipelines and asset operations.
- Enterprise lease accounting and compliance.
- GIS primitives, demographics, drive times, and trade areas.
- Foot-traffic datasets.
- Broker CRM and marketing production.
- Investment underwriting workflow.
- Generic document summarization.

The opportunity is more likely to arise through translation, orchestration, business context, and selective data partnerships than by reproducing each underlying system.

## 7. Business-Side Gaps

The gaps supported by the landscape audit are:

### 1. Pre-search diagnosis

Few general systems help a business determine whether real estate is the problem, what decision it faces, and when it must act.

### 2. Requirement intelligence for occasional occupiers

Enterprise tools model requirements, but there is no broadly established business-side standard that converts ordinary operational language into property, geography, economics, and diligence criteria.

### 3. Cross-property-type fit reasoning

Most marketplaces expose categorical filters. Business fit is conditional and compositional: “food production” depends on use, ventilation, drainage, utilities, loading, neighbors, buildout, and permitting—not a “kitchen” checkbox.

### 4. Integrated location-plus-space reasoning

Site-selection systems excel for repeat retail networks; marketplaces excel at inventory. The handoff from “where should this business operate?” to “which currently available environments fit?” remains fragmented.

### 5. Decision-quality comparison

Alternatives are commonly compared at the property or proposal level. Businesses need comparison at the operating-outcome level, including uncertainty.

### 6. Memory of rejected options and assumptions

Occupier products such as Tango and Occupier preserve portfolio and transaction information for professional teams. General search products rarely turn rejection reasons and prior decisions into reusable business intelligence.

### 7. Continuity between lease and market

Lease-administration systems know obligations; marketplaces know alternatives; operating systems know business performance. These domains are not normally joined for smaller occupiers.

### 8. Independent explanation

A business needs to understand why a location fits, what evidence is missing, which tradeoffs are subjective, and which claims require professional confirmation. Recommendation without auditable reasoning will not overcome CRE’s trust burden.

## 8. Residential Lessons—and Where the Analogy Breaks

### Lessons that transfer

Residential products demonstrate the value of:

- Broad, searchable inventory.
- Map-first exploration.
- Rich media and remote understanding.
- Saved searches and alerts.
- Preference learning.
- Neighborhood context.
- Affordability calculations.
- Comparable valuation.
- Low-friction connection to professionals.

Zillow’s natural-language search can interpret commute, affordability, schools, points of interest, and home attributes, then save and monitor the search. [Zillow AI search](https://investors.zillowgroup.com/news-and-events/news/news-details/2024/Zillows-AI-powered-home-search-gets-smarter-with-new-natural-language-features/default.aspx)

### Where the analogy breaks

| Residential | Commercial |
|---|---|
| Household preferences dominate. | Operating requirements can be hard constraints. |
| Bedrooms and bathrooms are relatively standardized. | “Suitable space” changes by business process and property subtype. |
| Public schools and neighborhood attributes are broadly reusable. | Zoning, infrastructure, loading, ventilation, and technical systems are parcel- and use-specific. |
| Purchase price or rent captures much of the comparison. | Buildout, pass-throughs, downtime, utilities, concessions, and obligations can dominate. |
| One household decides. | Finance, operations, HR, employees, customers, advisers, and regulators influence the decision. |
| Inspection follows a relatively established pattern. | Diligence depends heavily on the particular use and building. |
| MLS infrastructure standardizes much inventory. | CRE inventory and fields are more fragmented and selectively disclosed. |
| The dwelling’s use is generally known. | Legal and technical feasibility may remain unresolved late into the process. |

**Research inference:** The transferable residential model is consumer-grade clarity and continuity—not residential attribute search transplanted into CRE.

## 9. Travel / Other Market Lessons

### Travel

Travel platforms evolved from inventory access into decision systems through:

- Flexible discovery when the destination is unknown.
- Normalized comparison across suppliers.
- Real-time price and availability.
- Maps, photos, reviews, and surrounding context.
- Price history and alerts.
- Bundling of interdependent choices.
- Saved trips and persistent preferences.
- A handoff from inspiration to booking.

Google’s 2025–26 travel products can translate natural-language intent into options, combine real-time flight/hotel data with maps and reviews, explain tradeoffs, save plans, and monitor prices. [Google AI travel planning](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/agentic-plans-booking-travel-canvas-ai-mode/)

What does not transfer cleanly:

- Flights and rooms are highly structured, frequently repriced inventory.
- Commercial properties are heterogeneous and infrequently transacted.
- “Bookable” travel availability is much more reliable than “available” CRE space.
- CRE decisions have longer consequences and require legal, technical, and construction work after selection.

### Recruiting

Useful patterns from LinkedIn and Indeed:

- Translate employer requirements into candidate matching.
- Treat fit as multidimensional, not a single filter.
- Preserve profiles and histories.
- Explain candidate/job compatibility.
- Support a marketplace while retaining human interviews and negotiation.

CRE lesson: the requirement and the property both need structured profiles, while rejection and outcome data can improve future matching.

### Autos

CarGurus and AutoTrader illustrate:

- Normalize heterogeneous inventory.
- Put price in market context.
- Separate advertised price from condition, financing, ownership cost, and seller trust.
- Use alerts and deal-quality labels to reduce expert dependence.

CRE lesson: “good deal” must be contextualized against fit, total cost, condition, and uncertainty—not asking rent alone.

### Financial planning

Useful patterns:

- Start from goals, liabilities, time horizon, and risk tolerance.
- Maintain a persistent profile.
- Model scenarios rather than promise a single forecast.
- Monitor drift and trigger action when thresholds are crossed.

CRE lesson: real estate is a long-duration operating commitment and should be treated as a continuing planning problem.

### B2B procurement

Useful patterns:

- Guided specification building.
- Must-have versus negotiable criteria.
- Vendor evidence and certifications.
- Comparable total cost.
- Multi-stakeholder approvals.
- Audit trails and exception handling.

CRE lesson: fit evidence and decision governance matter more than a long ranked list.

## 10. AI Discontinuities

### Genuinely more practical since 2023

1. **Conversational requirement elicitation:** adaptive interviews can translate ordinary business language into structured criteria and identify contradictions or omissions.

2. **Unstructured document understanding:** leases, proposals, listing flyers, plans, inspection reports, zoning text, and emails can be extracted and cross-referenced at far lower marginal cost.

3. **Evidence-linked proposal comparison:** differing terms can be normalized with citations back to source documents.

4. **Natural-language exploration of complex data:** non-analysts can interrogate maps, leases, comps, and portfolio data without learning specialist query tools.

5. **Personalized explanation:** the same property can be explained differently to a CFO, COO, HR leader, or founder using the business’s own constraints.

6. **Persistent contextual memory:** requirements, assumptions, rejected options, and decision history can be retained and reused.

7. **Multimodal review:** photos, plans, maps, and documents can be examined together to flag questions, inconsistencies, or missing evidence.

8. **Scenario generation:** multiple headcount, attendance, growth, cost, or timing cases can be produced and revised interactively.

9. **Continuous monitoring:** agents can watch dates, market changes, new inventory, and threshold conditions.

10. **Workflow orchestration:** systems can request missing information, assemble comparison materials, coordinate follow-ups, and maintain an audit trail.

### Old workflows with a chatbot attached

- A listing search that merely converts prose into the same filters.
- A generic neighborhood summary assembled from public web pages.
- A lease summary with no source references or human verification.
- A site score with no business-specific model or transparent variables.
- A chat interface that cannot access live availability, business context, or authoritative documents.
- Automated outreach whose only goal is more broker or landlord leads.

JLL’s 2025 survey found CRE AI pilots had expanded dramatically, but maturity remained low; JLL characterizes successful programs as depending on business impact and existing technology/data maturity. [JLL Global Real Estate Technology Survey](https://www.jll.com/en-au/insights/global-real-estate-technology-survey)

## 11. AI Data Constraints

| Data domain | AI can do now | Remaining bottleneck |
|---|---|---|
| **Public geography/demographics** | Summarize, map, compare, create drive/transit areas, explain tradeoffs. | Update cadence, methodological differences, and whether proxies predict the specific business outcome. |
| **Business context** | Structure interviews and model scenarios. | Accuracy, permission, maintenance, and willingness to share sensitive plans. |
| **Listing data** | Deduplicate, extract attributes, match stated requirements. | Completeness, recency, shadow/off-market inventory, inconsistent fields. |
| **Property/building data** | Join records and flag likely fit. | Missing power, HVAC, loading, floor load, condition, divisibility, and landlord commitments. |
| **Zoning/permitted use** | Retrieve and summarize codes; identify likely constraints. | Local variation, overlays, interpretations, discretionary approvals, and unreliable digitization. |
| **Lease documents** | Extract dates, economics, clauses, and responsibilities with review. | Amendments, conflicting language, poor scans, legal interpretation, jurisdiction, and negotiated context. |
| **Market rents/comps** | Analyze supplied comps and explain ranges. | Effective terms are private; samples are biased and professional datasets proprietary. |
| **Buildout** | Create scope checklists and benchmark ranges. | Design, existing conditions, contractor market, code triggers, and schedule uncertainty. |
| **Operating costs** | Normalize known charges and scenarios. | Historical reconciliations, future capital items, utility use, tax reassessment, and responsibility allocation. |
| **Outcome data** | Learn from sales, utilization, hiring, or throughput when integrated. | Most businesses do not connect these outcomes to site decisions; causality is difficult. |

**Conclusion:** AI can reason well when the task is translating, synthesizing, comparing, explaining, or monitoring trustworthy evidence. It should not present certainty where the decisive fact has not been measured, disclosed, inspected, or approved.

## 12. Persistent Business Relationship

Real-estate responsibilities after signing include:

- Rent and operating-expense administration.
- Insurance and compliance obligations.
- Repair and maintenance responsibilities.
- Option, termination, renewal, expansion, contraction, and notice dates.
- Utilization and capacity.
- Customer, workforce, freight, and operating changes.
- Adjacent-space and sublease opportunities.
- Market-rent and concession changes.
- Landlord proposals and capital plans.
- Additional-location evaluation.
- Restoration and disposition planning.

Existing enterprise products prove these are durable jobs. Tango connects lease data, utilization, employee proximity, scenario planning, and future option decisions; Occupier connects transaction records to active leases and critical dates. [Tango Portfolio Strategy](https://tangoanalytics.com/products/tango-portfolio-strategy/), [Occupier Deal Management](https://www.occupier.com/product/deal-management)

The unresolved question is segment economics.

**Strategic hypothesis:** Smaller businesses will sustain a persistent relationship only if the system asks for little ongoing maintenance and reliably delivers value at infrequent but consequential moments. Lease dates alone may create a reminder utility; combining dates with current business conditions and market alternatives could create intelligence.

## 13. Business Real Estate Profile

A useful conceptual profile has four layers:

1. **Business:** industry, business model, operating process, customers, suppliers, growth, hours, risk tolerance.
2. **People and network:** employees, recruiting area, customers, deliveries, partners, existing locations.
3. **Physical and regulatory requirement:** activities, space zones, equipment, utilities, access, use, permits, security, special buildout.
4. **Decision and portfolio memory:** leases, costs, searches, assumptions, rejected alternatives, selected option, actual outcome.

Comparable profile models exist in:

- Recruiting: candidate and employer histories.
- Travel: traveler, trip, and loyalty preferences.
- Financial planning: goals, assets, liabilities, horizon, and risk.
- Procurement: specification, supplier, approval, and performance records.
- CRM: account, interactions, opportunities, and decisions.

The high-value attribute is not profile completeness. It is **progressive enrichment**: each interaction should improve future decisions without repeatedly interrogating the user.

Key risks include:

- Sensitive employee and customer locations.
- Confidential lease terms and expansion plans.
- Outdated assumptions presented as facts.
- Profiles becoming cumbersome to maintain.
- Using demand data in ways that disadvantage the contributing business.

## 14. Proactive Intelligence

A useful alert has four properties:

1. Material consequence.
2. Credible supporting data.
3. Enough lead time to act.
4. A clear next decision—not merely “engagement.”

### High-value alert candidates

- A renewal, option, termination, or restoration window is approaching.
- Headcount, attendance, storage, throughput, or sales indicates a capacity threshold may be crossed.
- A lease charge differs from the extracted obligation or historical pattern.
- Suitable adjacent or nearby expansion space becomes available.
- A previously rejected district or property changes materially.
- Current occupancy cost appears outside a defensible market range.
- Employee or customer geography has shifted enough to change location tradeoffs.
- Market conditions or competing supply materially alter stay-versus-go economics.
- A landlord proposal should be evaluated against alternatives before acceptance.
- A use, permit, infrastructure, or building-fit claim remains unverified as a deadline approaches.

### Notification-spam patterns

- Generic market news.
- Minor listing additions.
- Unsupported “your space is too expensive” claims.
- Repeated alerts without new evidence.
- Alerts whose only action is contacting a broker.
- Predictions based on stale business-profile fields.

## 15. Landlord Demand Opportunity

A landlord does not need another undifferentiated listing endpoint if existing channels already create exposure. Participation becomes rational when business-side demand supplies information that affects leasing decisions or asset strategy.

Potentially valuable proprietary signals include:

- Verified, time-bounded requirements by size, use, geography, timing, and credit profile.
- Demand that could fit the building but has not discovered it.
- Business-type demand at building or district level.
- Hard requirement mismatch rates.
- Common rejection reasons: layout, access, transit, parking, loading, power, image, cost, terms, or missing data.
- Relative competitive positioning against realistic substitutes.
- Demand for divisibility, turnkey condition, flexibility, or specialized improvements.
- Forecast demand not yet represented by broker inquiries.
- Whether providing one missing attribute or concession would materially increase qualified matches.
- Anonymized conversion from recommendation to inquiry, tour, proposal, and occupancy.

The strongest landlord value is not “more leads.” It is:

> Better-qualified demand, a clearer explanation of why demand is lost, and evidence for inventory, improvement, pricing, and positioning decisions.

Privacy must prevent landlords from using individual business context to extract rent or discriminate among prospects.

## 16. Marketplace Flywheel

```text
Business context
      ↓
Structured requirements → recommendations → behavior/outcomes
      ↑                                    ↓
better building data ← landlord value ← aggregated demand insight
```

### What must be true

- Requirement capture is easier than doing nothing.
- Recommendations outperform ordinary listing browsing on relevance and explanation.
- Availability and building data are sufficiently current.
- Businesses permit aggregated use of their behavior.
- Landlord insights are unique, actionable, and privacy-safe.
- Better landlord data measurably improves recommendations.
- The system learns from real fit and outcomes, not clicks alone.

### Chicken-and-egg problems

- Weak inventory data produces weak recommendations before landlord participation.
- Landlords see little reason to participate before demand is meaningful.
- Businesses do not provide deep context before experiencing value.
- Outcome data arrives months or years after recommendation.
- Brokers may possess key supply and deal information but resist disintermediation.

### Ways the flywheel could fail

- Demand records are vague, stale, duplicated, or broker-generated.
- Landlords optimize listings for matching rather than improve actual fit.
- Rejection reasons are inferred incorrectly.
- Privacy controls destroy granularity—or insufficient controls destroy trust.
- Marketplace monetization biases recommendations toward paying inventory.
- The product rewards inquiry volume rather than successful occupancy.

## 17. Broker Role

### Work likely to remain highly human

- Earning trust around consequential commitments.
- Local, tacit, and off-market knowledge.
- Access to landlords and other brokers.
- Reading motivation, credibility, and negotiating posture.
- Tours and real-time interpretation.
- Deal strategy and sequencing.
- Negotiation, concessions, and relationship management.
- Political navigation among executives and advisers.
- Coordinating legal, technical, construction, and landlord parties.
- Accountability when facts are incomplete.

### Work likely to be compressed or changed

- Initial requirement transcription.
- Broad inventory gathering.
- Reformatting flyers and surveys.
- Basic market narratives.
- First-pass proposal and lease comparisons.
- Repetitive follow-up.
- Deadline administration.
- Producing generic tour books and presentations.

If the business has better intelligence, the broker becomes less the exclusive container of information and more:

- Market access provider.
- Local interpreter.
- Negotiator.
- Exception handler.
- Transaction orchestrator.
- Adviser whose claims can be tested against transparent evidence.

Possible relationship models are not mutually exclusive:

- **Augment:** business and broker share a structured decision record.
- **Handoff:** intelligence forms the requirement, then routes to an appropriate human.
- **Neutral coordination:** the system preserves business interests while brokers execute.
- **Selective replacement:** automate low-judgment functions, not the relationship-intensive transaction.

**Research inference:** Treating brokers as irrelevant would ignore the importance of private information and execution. Treating them as the sole customer would recreate the current orientation.

## 21. Failure Modes

1. **Infrequency overwhelms retention.** Most businesses make major CRE decisions only every several years.

2. **Requirement capture feels like work.** Users may prefer browsing concrete listings to answering operational questions.

3. **Data quality undermines trust.** One stale availability or incorrect use assertion can outweigh many good recommendations.

4. **The business lacks its own answers.** Growth, attendance, customer origin, equipment needs, and budget may be genuinely uncertain.

5. **The system produces false precision.** Scores and rankings can hide subjective weights and missing facts.

6. **Listings remain the actual user demand.** Some users may value only inventory access and professional connection.

7. **Human advisers retain essential private information.** Technology may improve preparation without controlling transaction outcomes.

8. **The product becomes too broad.** Office, retail, medical, industrial, and life science have radically different diligence logic.

9. **Persistent profiles become stale.** Old assumptions can make personalization worse than a fresh interview.

10. **Privacy blocks the flywheel.** Employee geography, customer data, lease economics, and expansion plans are sensitive.

11. **Monetization corrupts recommendation neutrality.** Paid landlord placement could conflict with business fit.

12. **Landlord participation never becomes necessary.** Existing brokers and listing channels may remain sufficient.

13. **Scaled incumbents move downstream.** CoStar, Crexi, enterprise occupier platforms, brokerages, or general AI products can expose more consumer-friendly intelligence.

14. **Acquisition costs are too high.** Search traffic can be expensive, while transaction frequency and monetization may be low.

15. **Outcome learning is too slow.** Lease decisions take months and performance may take years to observe.

16. **Regulatory or professional-liability exposure rises.** Zoning, legal, engineering, and financial explanations may be mistaken for definitive advice.

17. **Small requirements receive poor market service.** Even well-qualified businesses may be economically unattractive to brokers or landlords.

18. **Recommendation does not cause action.** Users may appreciate analysis but revert to familiar districts, executive preference, or broker advice.

## 22. Ten Most Important Research Findings

1. **The fundamental underserved object is the business requirement, not the listing.**

2. **The strongest existing occupier technology serves enterprises and repeat multi-location operators, not occasional small and midsize occupiers.**

3. **Businesses commonly learn their real requirements during the search because operational needs are difficult to translate into CRE attributes.**

4. **Location choice and building choice are connected but distinct decisions; inventory-first products collapse them prematurely.**

5. **Total occupancy cost, technical fit, and permitted use are more consequential than base-rent and listing-attribute comparison.**

6. **The hardest CRE information problem is cross-domain interpretation: business, geography, property systems, regulation, construction, economics, and lease language.**

7. **AI now makes translation, document understanding, comparison, memory, explanation, and monitoring substantially cheaper—but cannot manufacture reliable availability, comps, or building facts.**

8. **Search does not end at lease execution. Renewal leverage, obligations, capacity, utilization, expansion, and market change create continuing jobs.**

9. **Brokers remain important where private information, relationships, negotiation, and exception handling matter; better business intelligence changes their role more plausibly than eliminating it.**

10. **The most defensible long-term asset would be a privacy-safe history of requirements, decisions, fit evidence, and outcomes—not a generic AI interface.**

## 23. Ten Questions Rofo Should Answer Before Creating a Long-Term Product Roadmap

1. Which business segment experiences enough CRE pain, frequency, and economic consequence to sustain a relationship beyond a single search?

2. At what point will a business provide operating context, and what immediate value earns each additional piece of data?

3. Which property types can be supported with credible requirement and fit reasoning, and which require specialized vertical expertise?

4. What evidence would demonstrate that location recommendations improve business decisions rather than merely increase engagement?

5. Which decisive data can Rofo obtain reliably—availability, building systems, zoning, costs, lease terms—and where must uncertainty remain explicit?

6. Can total occupancy cost be compared credibly before design and negotiation, and how should ranges and assumptions be represented?

7. How will the product remain neutral if landlords, brokers, or promoted inventory fund it?

8. What continuing event is valuable enough for businesses to maintain a profile between transactions?

9. What business-demand insight would landlords change behavior or pay to receive while preserving occupier privacy?

10. Which parts of the workflow should be self-service, professionally verified, broker-assisted, or explicitly outside the product’s authority?

## Final Question

> If commercial real estate technology were redesigned around the operating needs of the business occupying the space rather than around listings and transactions, what are the most important product opportunities that emerge?

The most important opportunities are:

1. **Business-to-requirement translation:** turning operating needs, people, customers, equipment, finances, timing, and uncertainty into a structured real-estate requirement.

2. **Location decision intelligence:** reasoning about markets and districts before presenting buildings, with explicit tradeoffs among workforce, customers, logistics, regulation, brand, and cost.

3. **Business-specific property fit:** interpreting each building against the proposed use—not simply matching listing fields—and identifying what remains unverified.

4. **Comparable decision economics:** normalizing rent, operating costs, concessions, buildout, disruption, flexibility, and risk into transparent scenarios.

5. **Evidence-linked decision support:** showing why an option fits, why another was rejected, what sources support the conclusion, and where professional validation is required.

6. **Persistent business real-estate memory:** retaining locations, leases, requirements, assumptions, preferences, rejected alternatives, and outcomes so each decision begins with accumulated context.

7. **Proactive planning between transactions:** monitoring dates, capacity, business change, market alternatives, and expansion or contraction signals early enough to preserve choice.

8. **Human-plus-software execution:** equipping the business with independent intelligence while using brokers, attorneys, architects, contractors, and engineers where relationships, negotiation, verification, and accountability remain essential.

9. **Demand intelligence for the supply side:** aggregating privacy-safe requirements and rejection reasons into information that gives landlords a rational reason to improve inventory data, building positioning, flexibility, and fit.

Taken together, these opportunities redefine the product from a place where a business finds space into a system that helps the business understand, choose, operate, and continually reconsider real estate as part of its operating model. That is the strategic opportunity landscape—not yet a conclusion about what Rofo should build.
