# Rofo Location Comparison Research Framework

This framework defines how Rofo should research future districts, subdistricts, and comparison pages when deep local knowledge is not already available. The goal is to support real commercial location decisions, not arbitrary SEO pairings.

## Purpose

Rofo comparison pages should help users understand whether one commercial environment fits their business better than another. A valid comparison should reflect a real leasing or location decision: office users choosing between downtown cores, flex users choosing between industrial corridors, lab users choosing between institutional clusters, or companies choosing between transit-oriented and highway-oriented settings.

The system should scale through structured commercial reasoning, historical Rofo data, public market evidence, light editorial review, and local review where available.

## Identifying Real Commercial Districts

A candidate district should have at least two of the following:

- recurring mention in brokerage market reports or submarket maps
- visible concentration of office, flex, warehouse, lab, retail, or mixed commercial buildings
- repeated appearance in Rofo historical listings, brochures, PDFs, or address clusters
- local government, economic development, BID, or planning recognition
- meaningful transportation logic such as BART, Caltrain, light rail, ferry, airport, port, highway, or arterial access
- a coherent business-location thesis that can be explained without hype

Avoid creating districts from vague residential neighborhood names unless there is a clear commercial concentration.

## Distinguishing Commercial Orientation

Office-oriented districts usually show:

- office building clusters, coworking supply, professional-service addresses, or institutional office anchors
- commute and client-access logic
- walkable downtown, transit, campus, or business-park context
- representative buildings that explain office scale and identity

Flex-oriented districts usually show:

- low-rise commercial buildings, service-commercial uses, loading potential, showroom/production uses, or office-warehouse formats
- arterial or highway access
- mixed office/industrial tenant language in listings and brochures
- less emphasis on polished client-facing identity

Warehouse-oriented districts usually show:

- larger parcels, loading, docks, truck access, highway/port/airport adjacency, yard or parking logic
- industrial brokerage language and recurring logistics users
- lower dependence on walkable amenities or district identity

Lab or life-science-oriented districts usually show:

- research institutions, hospitals, universities, incubators, lab-capable buildings, or specialist brokerage language
- modern buildings, larger floorplates, wet/dry lab references, or scientific tenant clusters
- proximity to institutional anchors that explain demand

Retail-oriented areas should be secondary for now. They require more local nuance around visibility, foot traffic, demographics, parking, co-tenancy, and street hierarchy than office and flex comparisons.

## Source Stack

Use multiple evidence types before publishing a district or comparison:

- brokerage market reports from CBRE, JLL, Cushman & Wakefield, Newmark, Colliers, Avison Young, Kidder Mathews, Lee & Associates, and regional firms
- city economic development pages and business attraction materials
- planning documents, specific plans, zoning summaries, and subarea plans
- business improvement district pages and downtown association materials
- transit maps, station context, ferry terminals, highway access, airport and port proximity
- Google Maps for commercial concentration, building form, and street-level context
- Rofo historical listing data, address clusters, building records, company/source metadata, PDFs, and brochures
- representative building candidates and image availability from Rofo's internal corpus

Do not rely on one source type. A brokerage report can confirm market vocabulary, while Rofo data can confirm actual address-level commercial depth.

## Publishability Standard

A district is publishable when it has:

- a clear commercial thesis
- at least one primary archetype
- enough address/building evidence to avoid sounding invented
- at least 3 credible representative building candidates, even if not all are public yet
- a contextual relationship to nearby districts or submarkets
- enough source confidence to describe office or flex fit without pretending to know tenant-level nuance

Hold the district as internal-only if it is mostly residential, too broad, too thin, or only exists as a brokerage submarket label without a clear user-facing location decision.

## Comparison Page Standard

A comparison page is worth creating when:

- users might plausibly choose between the two locations for the same requirement
- the contrast teaches a real commercial decision
- each side has a distinct best-fit and less-ideal-for profile
- both districts have sufficient model data and representative environment evidence
- the page can answer "choose A if" and "choose B if" without generic filler

Avoid comparisons that only exist because two places are nearby. Proximity is useful, but it is not enough.

## Confidence Levels

Use four confidence states:

- `generated`: assembled from structured Rofo data and obvious geography, not ready for public use without review
- `researched`: supported by public reports, planning documents, map review, and Rofo internal data
- `locally_reviewed`: reviewed by someone with market familiarity or direct local context
- `flagship`: locally reviewed, strong imagery/building support, strong relationship graph, and suitable as a model district

Public pages should generally be `researched` or better. `generated` comparisons can exist as internal drafts only.

## Subdistrict Comparison Handling

Deeper comparisons can be valuable, but only when both sides have real commercial meaning.

Potential examples:

- Hollis Street Corridor vs West Berkeley: valid if comparing East Bay flex/lab/adaptive industrial corridors
- Emeryville vs Berkeley: valid if comparing life-science, flex, office, and institutional-adjacent environments
- Alameda vs Oakland: valid if focused on waterfront/flex/service-commercial tradeoffs, not a generic city comparison
- Hayward vs Fremont: valid if focused on industrial, flex, logistics, or East Bay highway/warehouse geography
- Hayward vs Union City: valid if comparing adjacent industrial/logistics corridors
- Oakland vs Emeryville: valid if comparing downtown/urban office geography with smaller flex/life-science-adjacent context
- Oakland vs Walnut Creek: valid if comparing urban BART-centered East Bay office with suburban downtown/professional office context

Use subdistrict names when the commercial geography is specific. Avoid broad city comparisons unless the user decision is genuinely city-level.

## Guardrails

- Do not invent local authority from thin data.
- Do not publish pages where both sides sound interchangeable.
- Do not turn comparison pages into listings pages.
- Do not use rankings, scores, or fake precision.
- Do not overfit retail assumptions without traffic, visibility, co-tenancy, or demographic evidence.
- Do not create pages that require deep local expertise if that expertise is not available.
- Do not imply availability, completeness, or market coverage.

## Minimum Evidence Before Public Launch

Before launching a district or comparison, confirm:

- primary archetype and commercial thesis
- office/flex/warehouse/lab orientation as applicable
- at least 3 strong source signals across public reports, Rofo data, maps, planning materials, or brochures
- reviewed relationship logic to at least 2 nearby or alternative districts
- representative building candidates or address clusters
- clear best-fit and less-ideal-for guidance
- no obvious mismatch between public district boundaries and actual commercial concentration
- confidence level is `researched` or higher

## Recommended Workflow

1. Identify candidate districts from Rofo address clusters and external market vocabulary.
2. Classify each candidate by primary commercial orientation.
3. Gather source evidence and note uncertainty.
4. Draft a district thesis and best-fit profile.
5. Identify comparison candidates based on real tenant decisions.
6. Validate differentiation: building form, transit/highway access, tenant fit, price/commute implication, and commercial identity.
7. Assign confidence.
8. Publish only when the comparison can help a user make a location decision without generic filler.
